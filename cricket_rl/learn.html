A FIELD GUIDE TO LEARNING BY DOING

You don’t need to know RL
to take your first step.

Start by making a few decisions yourself. Then replace those decisions with a rule, a table, and eventually a neural policy. The environment stays the same.

01 · PLAY AND OBSERVE

The smallest useful experiment

  1. Open the playground and start Your first decision in the learning companion.
  2. Choose a line and length. Predict whether the outcome will be a dot ball, runs or a wicket.
  3. Play the delivery. Inspect observation → action → reward → next state.
  4. Repeat three times, then write a reflection. Could randomness explain what happened?

02 · KNOW WHAT THE AGENT CONTROLS

One world. Two decision makers.

Bowling agent Batting agent
Observation 57 values: batter, field, pitch, score and history 66 values: that context plus released delivery cue
Action Line × length · 9 choices Stance × direction × power · 45 choices
Reward 8 × wickets − total runs Total runs − 8 × wickets
Episode An innings ends at the ball limit, wicket limit or successful chase.

A dot ball means no runs and no wicket. An over contains six legal deliveries; wides and no-balls add extras without using a legal ball. In batting mode the line and length cue is shown after release. This is a tactical decision task, not video perception or muscle-level bat control.

Reward is a design choice. A wicket is worth eight in this benchmark. That does not mean a real cricket wicket always has that value.

03 · REPLACE YOUR DECISIONS WITH A POLICY

Playing, inference and training are different.

Manual play: you choose. Random: actions are sampled. Rule-based: a fixed rule chooses. Trained policy: a saved model chooses, without changing its weights.

Training repeatedly collects transitions and updates an agent. The browser does not secretly train a model every time you play. Use the project’s Python exercises for that step.

The browser demo includes small frozen Q-learning tables from a declared training seed. Compare them with the rules; being trained does not guarantee better play. The linked model card records their budget and limitations.

Start with a small Q table

python -m cricket_rl.course_experiment \
  --role batting --algorithm qlearning \
  --steps 8192 --output runs/my-first-agent

Q-learning keeps an estimate of future reward for state/action pairs. It balances trying unfamiliar actions (exploration) with choosing actions that seem useful (exploitation). The teaching implementation simplifies the observation into a small table, so some information is lost.

Then compare neural algorithms

pip install -e '.[train,dev]'
python -m cricket_rl.course_experiment \
  --role batting --algorithm ppo \
  --steps 8192 --output runs/my-ppo-agent

Try --algorithm dqn and --role bowling with the same transition budget. PPO learns a policy from rollouts. DQN learns action values using replayed transitions. A larger network or a newer algorithm does not guarantee a better result.

04 · COMPARE FAIRLY

One great replay is not a result.

  • Change one condition at a time. Keep the rest fixed.
  • Use multiple training seeds and many evaluation innings.
  • Track reward, runs, wickets and balls; they expose different trade-offs.
  • Compare against random and rule-based baselines. Keep failed training seeds.
  • Tune on validation data. Keep a fresh final test set.

The playground’s snapshots are for exploration. Different action sequences consume random numbers differently, even with the same seed. Partial innings also have different exposure. Do not use two snapshots to claim statistical superiority. 

python -m cricket_rl.course_study \
  --steps 8192 --episodes 50 --seeds 0 1 2 \
  --output runs/course-study

This runs Q-learning, PPO and DQN for both roles. Full exercises, measured pilot results and API examples live in docs/course/START_HERE.md in the repository.

05 · SHARE WHAT YOU DISCOVER

A good learning artifact includes the failures.

Download a learning journal from the playground. It includes settings, actions, outcomes, predictions and your reflection. Saved snapshots stay in your browser; journals are files you choose to download. Do not put private information in a journal you intend to share.

When sharing a result, include your hypothesis, environment settings, algorithm, budget, seeds, baselines and limitations. Ask someone else to reproduce it.

This is an uncalibrated research simulator. Its physics and batting-response parameters are illustrative. It does not establish real-world cricket accuracy or state-of-the-art performance. The local server has no accounts or production sharing features; do not expose it publicly without a deployment review.
